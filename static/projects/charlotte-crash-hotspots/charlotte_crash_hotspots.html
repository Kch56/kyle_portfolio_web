Where Will Charlotte Crashes Cluster Next Month?

Forecasting reported crash counts in 500-meter map cells, with a closer look at morning and afternoon hotspots.

1. Problem definition

A crash can happen anywhere, but reported crashes are not spread evenly across Charlotte. I wanted to know whether a model could use previous months to identify the areas that would have the most crashes in the next one.

Research question: Can past crash patterns predict which 500-meter areas of Charlotte will have higher reported crash counts next month? I also asked whether separate morning and afternoon forecasts would improve the hotspot map.

This is a regression problem. Each row represents one map cell and one calendar month, and the target is the number of distinct reported crashes in that cell-month. A model produces an expected count for each cell. I then rank those counts to identify a small set of areas for closer attention. The project does not predict the exact location or time of an individual crash.

Transportation analysts and planners could use a ranking like this to decide where to look more closely. It is an exploratory aid, not a recommendation to change enforcement or allocate resources on its own.

2. Background and context

The Federal Highway Administration describes network screening as a way to combine crash history with roadway and traffic information to identify locations worth investigating (FHWA, n.d.-b). That supports the idea of ranking locations, but it also points to information missing from this first model: traffic volume and road characteristics.

Weather was another possible factor. FHWA reports that rain and wet pavement can increase driving risk (FHWA, n.d.-a). I explored daily precipitation from NOAA's Charlotte Douglas Airport station (National Centers for Environmental Information, n.d.). A measured wet day, however, is information from that day. It cannot be used as an observed feature in a forecast made the month before.

The City of Charlotte's crash layer supplies the dates, locations, and IDs used here (Charlotte Department of Transportation, n.d.). This project tests whether those records alone can improve on a simple history-based forecast. It is a student modeling exercise, rather than an official City of Charlotte safety analysis.

4. Understanding the data

The yearly totals change over the study period, and the hourly chart shows particularly high reported counts on weekday afternoons. Neither chart adjusts for how much people drove at those times. They show when crashes were recorded, not a driver's per-trip risk.

The daily weather comparison found an average of 107.2 crashes on 1,729 dry days and 109.6 crashes on 828 wet days. That small difference does not show that rain caused more crashes. It also cannot tell us whether it rained at the place and hour of each crash.

After grouping crashes into cells, 4,314 distinct cells had at least one crash in 2018–2024. The fixed study area uses the 4,146 cells seen by the end of 2022. It contains 348,264 cell-month rows from January 2018 to December 2024, including 240,867 zero-crash rows, about 69%. That many zeros make an overall average error easy to misread, so I also check how well the model ranks busier areas.

5. Preparation and feature selection

I converted the crash timestamp to Charlotte local dates, filtered coordinates, assigned each point to a 500-meter square using projected X/Y coordinates, and counted distinct crash IDs by cell and month. I selected cells using data available before 2023, then added rows with zero crashes for every month in each selected cell. This keeps the evaluation area consistent without choosing cells based on future test-year crashes.

The history features are shifted by one month, so the model cannot read the count it is trying to predict. The first three months lack sufficient lag history; modeling starts in April 2018. No category encoding is needed for the main features, and neither linear regression nor the decision tree in this notebook requires feature scaling. Linear predictions below zero are clipped to zero because negative crash counts have no practical meaning.

The training period is April 2018–December 2022; 2023 is for comparing models and settings; and 2024 is the evaluation year. This is a rolling monthly forecast. For example, the December 2024 prediction may use observed crashes through November 2024, as a monthly analyst would. The 2024 evaluation therefore does not mean all 12 months were predicted once from the end of 2023.

6. Baseline and model development

The baseline simply predicts the average number of crashes in that cell over its previous three months. It sets a useful bar because a model should do more than repeat recent history. I trained a decision tree regressor, which can split cells into groups using nonlinear rules, and a linear regression model, which combines the numeric features to estimate a count. Both use the same target, study cells, and date splits.

The first tree, using only a one-month count and a three-month average, had a 2023 mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.618, worse than the baseline's 0.585. I checked several tree depths and minimum leaf sizes on 2023, then added a previous-12-month average. With a maximum depth of 14 and at least 300 rows per leaf, the longer-history tree reached 0.571. The same longer-history features gave linear regression 0.565 on 2023, so I selected linear regression as the main model.

Evaluation history: The notebook displayed a 2024 tree result before linear regression was added. I then compared linear regression on the same 2024 split. No 2024 rows were included in model fitting, but it would overstate the process to call the entire modeling sequence a fully blind final test. The 2023 comparison and the saved notebook record the sequence.

7. Evaluation and model selection

MAE is the average absolute difference between predicted and observed counts per cell-month. A lower MAE means the average prediction is closer. To evaluate the hotspot goal, I also take the top 5% of cells by predicted count in each month and calculate their share of that month's actual crashes. A higher share means the ranking places more crashes in the highlighted areas.

8. What the model learned

The prior 12-month average was the most useful feature in a permutation test: shuffling it raised MAE by about 0.618. The three-month average raised MAE by 0.066 and last month's count by 0.017. Calendar month and the grid coordinates added little once crash history was present. These lag features overlap, so their permutation scores do not measure independent causal effects.

The model struggles more when a cell-month is busy. Among 2,595 cell-months with at least five crashes, the average actual count was 7.84 but the average prediction was 6.38, and MAE was 2.45. In contrast, the 33,628 zero-crash cell-months had MAE of 0.27. The overall MAE therefore should not be read as typical accuracy at a busy hotspot.

9. Morning and afternoon extension

The 276,118 retained records all had usable times. I counted crashes separately in the morning (6–11 a.m.) and afternoon (noon–6 p.m.), then rebuilt previous-month features for each cell and period. Crashes in other hours remain in the all-day analysis. On the 2024 period forecasts, linear regression improved MAE from 0.256 to 0.253 in the morning and from 0.374 to 0.368 in the afternoon relative to each period's three-month baseline. Its top 5% of cells captured 38.3% of morning crashes and 42.0% of afternoon crashes.

The key comparison is with the original all-day linear model. Its same top-ranked cells captured 38.5% of morning and 41.9% of afternoon crashes. The separate period models did not meaningfully improve the hotspot rankings, so I keep the all-day monthly model as the main result.

For December alone, the selected morning cells contained 406 of 1,058 crashes (38.4%); the selected afternoon cells contained 855 of 2,009 (42.6%). These are counts captured by a shortlist of areas, not the percentage of crash locations guessed exactly.

10. Limitations, ethics, and reflection

Exposure and geography. A high crash count can reflect a busy road rather than a higher crash rate per driver. The dataset lacks a traffic-volume denominator, so I cannot compare personal risk across neighborhoods. The coordinate filter is a plausible Charlotte-area rectangle rather than a formal city boundary, and the fixed 2022 cell set excludes cells first observed later. The airport's daily weather does not measure conditions at each road and hour.

Prediction errors. A cell ranked low that later has many crashes is a practical false negative for hotspot screening; it might not receive further attention. A cell ranked high that stays quiet is a practical false positive and could waste limited staff time. These are screening terms, not labels from a binary classification model. The underprediction in the busiest cells is a particular concern if a predicted count is taken literally.

Responsible use. Crash reports can reflect differences in recording practices or location accuracy. A map of past reports should not be treated as proof that residents in a particular place drive less safely, or as a sole basis for enforcement. A real planning workflow would pair these results with traffic exposure, street design, field review, and community input. A next study could add road segments, traffic counts, and archived weather forecasts, then test on later years without revisiting them during model selection.

Answer to the research question: Yes, previous crash counts help identify where future reported crashes will cluster. The gain over a straightforward history baseline is small, and a time-of-day model added little to the all-day hotspot ranking. The result supports prioritizing areas for investigation, not forecasting individual crashes.
